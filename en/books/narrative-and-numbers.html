Central thesis

Every assumption in your spreadsheet must trace back to one sentence in your story. Every sentence in your story must convert to one number. Valuation is the bridge — not a spreadsheet, not a poem.

Damodaran opens Narrative and Numbers with a familiar observation: from middle school, people split into two tribes — storytellers (literature, history, philosophy) and number crunchers (math, engineering, accounting). Each side learns to distrust the other. By MBA time the gap looks unbridgeable.

Damodaran's point: both sides are wrong. Storyteller without discipline → confirmation bias, hype, fantasy valuations (Twitter IPO $40B on a meaningless user metric). Number cruncher without story → false precision, a DCF with 50 assumptions nobody can defend. "Numbers without narrative are exercises in financial modeling. Narratives without numbers are fairy tales." This book teaches you to build the bridge between the two tribes — and why you have to walk it in both directions.

Damodaran's 6-step is academic-clean — Vietnam SME reality differs

Damodaran assumes Twitter-level public data: 10-K filings, 5+ years of audited history, listed peer comparables. Vietnamese SMEs in 2024 have none of this. No 10-K. No audit. Founder revenue forecast is a one-line email: "growth 30%". Listed comparables in the same sector = zero. You're forced to build story from founder gossip, supplier checks, walking the factory floor and counting delivery trucks.

I think the Damodaran framework is 100% valid — the story → numbers → feedback flow is right. But the chapter weighting is wrong for emerging markets. Damodaran spends 30% of the book on story-building (chapters 6-7), 50% on numbers (chapters 8-10), 20% on feedback. The Vietnam SME deal I led in Q7 2024: 60% of time gathering story (8 stakeholder calls), 40% modeling. The ratio is fully inverted.

Skin in game Q7 2024 SME deal I led: founder pitched revenue forecast "+30%/year for 5 years". I didn't trust the number — I rebuilt the story. Three weeks of calls with 3 enterprise customers, 2 distributors, 1 supplier, 2 ex-employees. Real story: one customer = 45% of revenue, contract ends 2025, currently in an RFP with a competitor. Forecast rebuilt as 3 scenarios (channel-by-channel, customer cohort, capacity ceiling). Original DCF $X → my number $0.4X. Walked away. Damodaran process valid; for Vietnam the weights have to flip.
Framework 01 / The Story ↔ Numbers Bridge

Every sentence in the story → one cell in the spreadsheet

Damodaran insists: "every qualitative input — culture, brand, management — can and should be connected to a value driver." No exceptions. If you can't turn "amazing founder" into a number, it's not an insight — it's superstition.

SIDE 01 · Story

The Uber story, 2014

  • Uber is an urban car service, primarily replacing taxis
  • Networking benefits are local — not global
  • Competes with the taxi industry, not with rental cars
  • Takes 20% revenue share on transactions
  • Needs infrastructure investment to scale (drivers, ops)
  • Capital intensive — must raise capital multiple times
BRIDGE 1-to-1 mapping
SIDE 02 · Numbers

DCF inputs from the story

  • Total addressable market: $100B (urban taxi only)
  • Market share target: 10% (local network advantage)
  • Revenue slice: 20%
  • Growth rate years 1-5: 60%, decaying to 5% by year 10
  • Operating margin steady-state: 40% (asset-light)
  • Reinvestment rate: high in years 1-5 (capital raises)
  • Damodaran's value June 2014: $5.9B (vs VC $17B)
Lesson The Uber-as-urban-taxi story → 6 concrete numbers in the DCF. Damodaran's value $5.9B; VCs price $17B. Same numerical machinery, different story → value differs 3x. Eighteen months later (2015-2016), Damodaran switched the story (Uber-as-logistics, global), and value jumped to $24B. Story shifts → numbers shift → value shifts. There is no "objective" valuation — only valuation consistent with a story you can defend.
Framework 02 / The 6-step process

Story → Numbers → Value — and the feedback loop

Damodaran's 6-step bridge isn't linear — it's a loop. New market data, quarterly earnings, critics' feedback → return to revise the story → re-run the numbers → iterate. People who keep the story unchanged in the face of reality aren't investors — they're priests.

  • 1
    S
    Survey the company & the market
    Read history, business model, competitors. Damodaran: 80% of a valuator's time should be here — not in Excel. Understand first.
  • 2
    D
    Develop the narrative
    Write one paragraph answering 5 questions: what business, market size, market share path, profit margin, capital needs. Must be simple, credible, defendable.
  • 3
    T
    Test with the 3P framework
    Is your story Possible (rule out impossible)? Plausible (rule out unreasonable)? Probable (likely)? Many stories are possible but improbable — cut them.
  • 4
    C
    Convert narrative to value drivers
    Each sentence in the story → one input: market size → revenue cap, market share → growth, competitive intensity → margin, capital intensity → reinvestment.
  • 5
    V
    Value the company (DCF)
    Plug drivers into the DCF. Sensitivity analysis on the 2-3 most uncertain inputs. Output: a number plus a range. Not a single point estimate.
  • 6
    F
    Feedback loop
    Listen to critics. New data, earnings reports, market shifts → return to step 2 (Develop). The story stays alive, not frozen. Most quants skip this step — fatal.
Framework 03 / Story arc by Lifecycle

Startup stories differ from Mature stories differ from Decline stories

Damodaran's key insight: story weight shifts with company stage. Early Startup → 90% story / 10% numbers (no history to anchor on). Mature → flips to 30/70. Story-stage mismatch = the classic mispricing. Twitter 2013: priced as Mature (P/E comparable) but the business was Startup → wrong frame → an absurd $40B IPO.

STAGE 01
Startup
Story 90%Num 10%
No revenue, no history. Value = a story about the future. Vast disagreement between investors is normal — because their stories are wildly different. High volatility.
"Stories are the only thing — but they must pass the 3P test. Most fail Probable."
STAGE 02
Growth
Story 65%Num 35%
Revenue ramping, margins improving. Story still drives value (TAM, share). Numbers start backing or breaking the story. Disagreement narrows.
"Numbers begin testing the story. If revenue trajectory diverges → story break."
STAGE 03
Mature
Story 30%Num 70%
Stable cash flow, predictable margins. Numbers dominate. Story = the thread keeping numbers coherent. Investor disagreement narrows — most argue about terminal value.
"Story = capital allocation choice + competitive moat duration."
STAGE 04
Decline
Story 50%Num 50%
Revenue declining, but cash thrown off. Story = endgame: liquidate? Pivot? Run-off? Numbers count the cash. Equal weighting returns — story decides which numbers matter.
"Endgame story — return capital, pivot, or slow death? Each implies a different value."

Vietnam context: VinFast's 2023 SPAC IPO at $23B = priced as Mature (Tesla revenue multiple). Reality: Startup stage, no history, 90% story. When the story broke a year later (production, demand) → drop -90%. The mismatch between life-cycle stage and pricing frame was predictable. VNM 2024 is the inverse: Mature stage (30% story / 70% numbers), but the market priced mostly the story (lawsuit, management) → bias opportunity to buy when the numbers were still intact.

Building a Narrative

Test-Driving a Narrative — 3P Test

Improving & Modifying — Feedback Loop
Listen to critics: who disagrees? Why? Adjust the story incrementally. Most quants treat feedback as attack — wrong. The best valuators iterate weekly.

Narrative Alterations — Real World Intrudes

News and Narratives

Go Big — The Macro Story

The Corporate Life Cycle
