The Most Important Thing.

Successful investing isn't about doing the right thing — it's about avoiding the wrong thing. Howard Marks (founder of Oaktree, $190B AUM) condenses four decades of memos into 21 "most important things". Each one = a lens for seeing real risk, second-level thinking, refusing the herd. Plural — there is no single thing. Counter for retail: cycle-timing edge needs a structural advantage retail doesn't have.

SECOND-LEVEL
Central thesis

Successful investing isn't doing the right thing — it's avoiding the wrong thing. Edge for the thoughtful investor: think differently and better.

Marks wrote The Most Important Thing as an essay collection — condensing 20+ years of Oaktree memos into 21 "most important things". Background: Marks cofounded Oaktree Capital in 1995, specialty distressed debt + credit cycle. Investing style: defensive first, focus on avoiding loss instead of maximising gain. Core: there is no single thing — plural — because markets are complex and each situation needs a different lens.

Three core frameworks: Second-Level Thinking (first-level: "good company → buy"; second-level: "everyone already thinks it's good → priced in → not an opportunity"); Pendulum (markets aren't in equilibrium — they swing greed ↔ fear extreme; buy at fear extreme, sell at greed extreme); Risk = Permanent Loss (NOT volatility; β is meaningless; risk only realised in retrospect). Plus: Defensive Investing (compound math: -50% needs +100% to recover) and Cycles + Reflexivity (credit cycle drives everything). I applied this partially: 2018-2021 held cash waiting for "blood" → cost of patience high; 2022 deployed at -25% → +18% recovered; Q4/2024 passed an SME deal because of late-cycle position.

"
Investing is a popularity contest, and the most dangerous thing is to buy something at the peak of its popularity.
Howard Marks, The Most Important Thing

Marks writes for institutional capital that can withstand 5-10 years illiquid plus 50% drawdown — retail VN doesn't have that structural advantage

Marks at Oaktree is a credit-distressed expert. The "buy when blood is in the streets" style assumes three things: (1) you have cash to deploy when blood appears, (2) your employer doesn't lay you off in the same crisis, (3) your family doesn't need money at the same time. In 2008-2009, US retail investors mass-redeemed at the bottom — not foolish, that was survival. Marks deployed billions in 2009; US retail panic-sold IRAs → both rational at their structural level. Marks's edge isn't insight — it's structural advantage: 5-10 year capital lockups, cheap leverage, a 50+ analyst team, direct phone calls with CEOs. Retail investors don't have 1/10 of that.

Three axes where Vietnam (and most retail) differs from Marks's assumptions: (1) Liquidity asymmetry — the cash you need during a crisis (job loss, family need) usually coincides with market lows; (2) No cheap leverage — Marks borrows at 3-5%, retail margin in VN at 12-15% can't be held through a 12-month drawdown; (3) Information asymmetry — Marks calls CEOs and learns the real balance sheet; retail reads public reports six months late. Adapt Marks: borrow the framework (second-level thinking, defensive math, asymmetric recovery) — but not the timing edge. DCA steadily + 12-month spending cash buffer + only deploy when drawdown >25% with small portions <30% NAV per tranche.

Skin in game 2018-2021 I held 50% cash waiting for "blood" using Marks's framework — Marks wrote a 2017-2018 memo warning "the seven worst words: too much money chasing too few deals". Reality: the VN-Index bull continued for three more years and I missed roughly 80% of the gains. Cost of patience: if that 50% cash had been DCA'd into the index it would be +60-80%; I made 4-5%. Lesson: Marks's macro call was right (late cycle) but timing was wrong by three years — institutions can take three years of "looking stupid", retail capital sitting idle is too costly. In the 2022 crash I deployed the 50% cash buffer at -25% drawdown → +18% recovery in 18 months. Marks's framework worked — when the wave finally arrived. Q4/2024 I passed an SME deal with an 18% IRR because of late-cycle position + 4× leverage on the balance sheet — Marks lens: late cycle = expensive risk even at attractive pricing.
Framework 01 / Second-Level Thinking

First-level vs second-level — five stock examples

Marks chapter 1: "Your goal isn't to earn average returns; you want to do better than average." First-level thinking is easy — anyone can do it — so it doesn't create edge. Second-level thinking: think about what others are thinking, what's already priced in, and where you might be wrong. Edge = thinking what others missed. If you reach the same conclusion as consensus → consensus already priced it in → no opportunity.

Level 1 · Surface · Consensus
Level 2 · Deep · What's priced in?
Apple · 2024
"Apple is a great company, the strongest brand in the world, services growing. Buy."
Apple · L2
"Apple is great — but EVERYONE knows Apple is great. P/E 30× already prices in 8-10% growth/year for the next 5 years. If growth comes in at only 5% → multiple compresses → -30%. The right question: what does the market not yet know?"
Tesla · 2021
"Tesla is disrupting automotive + energy + AI. EV growth is massive. Musk is a visionary. Buy."
Tesla · L2
"The Tesla narrative is consensus. P/E 1000× discounts 20+ years of growth. If BYD + legacy auto can compete — multiple collapses 80%. Risk = price-of-perfection. Skip even if the story is right."
VNM · 2018
"Vinamilk dominates with 50% market share, strong brand, 6% dividend. Buy and hold forever."
VNM · L2
"Dominance is already priced into a P/E of 22× — discounting 8% mature growth. But plant-based + competitor TH True Milk + dairy import tariff cuts are coming. Margins compress → growth 4%. Real low in 2022 -45%. L2 sells in 2018, buys back in 2022."
HPG · 2020
"Hoa Phat steel growth, capacity expanding at Dung Quat 2. EBITDA rising. Buy."
HPG · L2
"Cyclical at peak. Low P/E = peak earnings, not cheap. Steel price $700/ton = 10-year high. Mean reverts → margins halve → EPS halves → P/E 12× → 24× at lower price. L2: avoid peak cyclicals. The 2022 trip to $400/ton steel → -55%."
ACB · 2022 (-50%)
"Bank crisis Van Thinh Phat + interest rate spike. Bond market frozen. Sell — risk too high."
ACB · L2
"The Van Thinh Phat risk is SCB-specific, NOT ACB. Market panic is indiscriminate. ACB P/B 0.9, ROE 22%, NPL 0.7%. Discount for a risk that doesn't exist. L2: max-courage buy NOW while everyone sells. +85% in 14 months."
Framework 02 / Pendulum Swing

Markets aren't in equilibrium — they swing between greed and fear

Marks chapter 9: "The mood swings of the securities markets resemble the movement of a pendulum." The pendulum spends very little time at the midpoint — mostly it swings between two extremes: euphoria-depression, optimism-pessimism, risk tolerance-risk aversion. Buy when the pendulum is at the fear extreme. Sell at the greed extreme. The catch: the most painful moment is the best entry. The most comfortable moment is the worst entry. Counter-intuitive, so few people do it.

1999 · Greed peak
Dotcom euphoria
Pets.com IPO at $400M with no revenue. Sector P/Es 200×+. "This time is different". Marks's 2000 memo bubble.com — sold heavy. Crash -78% NASDAQ over the next two years.
2009 · Fear peak
GFC trough
Bank insolvency, retail panic. S&P 666. Distressed credit yields 20%+. Marks's Oaktree deployed $25B in 2008-09. +88% S&P in the next 12 months.
2021 · Greed peak
COVID rally
SPAC mania, meme stocks (GME, AMC), $3T crypto, ARK 200×. "Stocks always go up". VN-Index 1500. Marks's Q4/21 memo warning. -50% NASDAQ + -34% VN-Index in 2022.
2022 · Fear peak
Trough · buy
VN-Index 900 (-40%), bank ACB P/B 0.9, real estate -60%. Van Thinh Phat + frozen bond market. I deployed 50% cash in Q4/22. +50% VN-Index in the next 18 months. Painful entry.
Framework 03 / Asymmetric Loss Math

-50% needs +100% to recover — recovery math is NOT symmetric

Marks chapters 7 and 17: "Avoiding losses is more important than scoring big wins." Compound math is asymmetric — losses hurt more than gains help. Lose 10% and you need +11% to recover. Lose 50% and you need +100%. Lose 75% and you need +300%. Defensive investing = focus on avoiding -50%, not maximising +100%. Because +100% after -50% only gets you back to break-even — NOT a win. Two investors: A returns +12%/year for 10 years = 3.1×. B returns +20% for 9 years and -50% in 1 year = 2.6×. Steady wins.

Apply: avoiding a -50% drawdown is more important than catching +100% upside. My personal position-sizing rules: no single position >15% NAV, no leverage >1.3×, exit at -25% on any individual position (hard stop). I held 30% of NAV in a single real-estate stock in 2018 → -45% in Q1/2020 from COVID → 24 months to recover, opportunity cost extremely high. After 2020 I enforce the 15% rule strictly.

21 most important things — condensed into 4 parts

Map of The Most Important Thing

Marks wrote 21 chapters = 21 "most important things". Plural — there is no single thing. I condense them into 4 PARTS by practical arc: Foundations (think differently, market efficiency, value); Risk (understand, recognise, control, avoid loss); Cycles (cycle awareness, pendulum, contrarian, bargains); Discipline (knowing what you don't know, defence, patience, expectations).

PART 1 · Foundations — Things 1-4
Think differently. Value over price. Edge from asymmetric insight.
Second-level thinking. Market (in)efficiency. Value. Price-value gap.
← Slide horizontally →
THING 01
Second-Level Thinking
First-level: "good company → buy". Second-level: "everyone knows it's good → priced in → sell". Edge = thinking what consensus missed. If you reach the same conclusion as consensus → no opportunity. Marks: "Your goal isn't average returns — you want better than average. Your thinking has to be different and at a higher level."
Edge
THING 02
Understanding Market (In)Efficiency
EMH is true for US large-cap — not for every market. Inefficient markets = where edge is possible: small-cap, distressed, emerging market, illiquid. Marks: "I don't waste time picking S&P stocks — I index. Active money goes into distressed credit where it's inefficient."
Inefficiency edge
THING 03
Value
Two schools: value (cheap vs intrinsic) and growth (future cash flow discount). Marks is value school. Discipline: establish intrinsic value INDEPENDENT of price. Buy when market price << intrinsic. Sell when price >> intrinsic. "It's not what you buy, it's what you pay."
Intrinsic value
THING 04
Price & Value Relationship
The same asset can be a bargain or a rip-off — depending on the price you paid. Apple at $90 = bargain, at $300 = rip-off. "No asset is so good it can't become a bad investment if bought too high. Few assets are so bad they can't be a good investment when bought low enough."
Price matters
PART 2 · Risk — Things 5-7
Risk = permanent loss, NOT volatility — define, recognise, control
Understand risk. Recognise risk. Control risk.
← Slide horizontally →
THING 05
Understanding Risk
Academic finance: risk = volatility = β. Marks: RISK = PROBABILITY OF PERMANENT LOSS. Volatility ≠ risk — bond zigzags don't matter if you hold to maturity. Risk is the chance you can't recover. β is meaningless for the long-term investor. "Risk only realises in retrospect — until the rare event happens, it looks safe."
Permanent loss
THING 06
Recognising Risk
Risk is highest when everyone thinks there is no risk. Counter-intuitive: perceived safety = real danger. 2007 mortgage AAA — perceived risk-free → priced for perfection → real risk highest. 2009 distressed bonds yielding 20% — perceived risky → priced for disaster → real risk LOW.
Perception flip
THING 07
Controlling Risk
You can't eliminate risk — you can only control it. Methods: (1) position size (no single position >15%), (2) diversification (across asset class + cycle), (3) margin of safety (buy 30%+ below intrinsic), (4) avoid leverage in late cycle. Marks's Oaktree slogan: "If we avoid the losers, the winners take care of themselves."
Risk control
PART 3 · Cycles — Things 8-12
Cycles + pendulum + contrarian — buy fear, sell greed
Cycles. Pendulum. Negative influences. Contrarian. Bargains.
← Slide horizontally →
THING 08
Being Attentive to Cycles
Markets don't go in straight lines — credit cycle drives everything. Easy money → bad lending → losses → tight money → no liquidity → forced sale → bargains → repeat. Marks's mantra: "We may never know where we're going, but we'd better have a good idea where we are."
Where are we?
THING 09
Awareness of Pendulum
Markets aren't in equilibrium — the pendulum swings greed ↔ fear, rarely at the midpoint. Buy at the fear extreme, sell at the greed extreme. Most painful = best entry. 1999/2009/2021/2022 all extreme. Reading sentiment = reading the pendulum's position.
Pendulum
THING 10
Combating Negative Influences
Psychology destroys investors: greed, fear, herd, ego, capitulation. Most lethal: capitulation at extremes (sell the bottom, buy the top). Defence: written investment plan, position rules, automatic rebalance. Don't trust intuition at pendulum extremes — intuition pushes you to herd.
Anti-herd
THING 11
Contrarianism
"To be a successful contrarian, you have to be willing to look wrong for a long time." Contrarian ≠ always opposite — contrarian = independent thinker. Buying at the bottom requires courage when consensus says doom. Selling at the top requires discipline when consensus says nothing can stop. Marks: in 2007 sold heavy, looked stupid for a year. Recovered.
Look wrong long
THING 12
Finding Bargains
Bargain characteristics: little known, fundamentally questionable on the surface, controversial/unappealing, declining, stigmatised. Things institutional investors CAN'T buy (mandate, size, reputation) → that's where bargains live. Distressed bonds, frontier markets, fallen angels.
Where institutions can't go
PART 4 · Discipline — Things 13-21
Patience + humility + defence + reasonable expectations
Patient opportunism. Knowing what you don't know. Where we stand. Luck. Defence. Pitfalls. Adding value. Reasonable expectations. Pulling it all together.
← Slide horizontally →
THING 13
Patient Opportunism
Don't search for opportunity — wait for it to come. The best investments are when the market hands them to you (panic). Most of the time, do nothing. Patience = competitive edge because most investors can't sit on cash for 12-24 months. Marks: "Wait for the pitch."
Wait for pitch
THING 14
Knowing What You Don't Know
Two schools: "I know" (forecast macro, time the market) — most popular, most wrong. "I don't know" (humble, focus on what's controllable). Marks is "I don't know" school. Don't predict — prepare for a range of outcomes. Size positions to survive the worst case.
Humility
THING 15
Sense for Where We Stand
You can't predict — but you can READ the current temperature. Marks's checklist: credit easy/tight? Pundits bullish/bearish? IPOs hot/cold? Risk premium high/low? Position sizing reflects temperature: aggressive when fearful, defensive when greedy. Not market timing — risk calibration.
Read temperature
THING 16
Appreciating the Role of Luck
Result = skill + luck. Single year: mostly luck. 10+ years: skill emerges. Don't conflate good outcome with good decision. You could be right for the wrong reason. Process > outcome. Same decision can produce different outcomes — judge by process quality, not result.
Process > outcome
THING 17
Investing Defensively
Two ways to win: (1) score lots of points (offence), (2) prevent the opponent scoring (defence). Marks picks defence. Avoiding -50% drawdown is more important than chasing +100% upside. Compound math is asymmetric. Steady -5% to +20% beats volatile -50% to +60% over decades.
Defence first
THING 18
Avoiding Pitfalls
Pitfalls Marks lists: imagination failure (didn't think it could happen), over-reliance on history (this time IS different sometimes), over-extending at peak, wishful thinking overriding evidence. Defence: pre-mortem (imagine failure scenarios), diversify, never go all-in on a single thesis.
Pre-mortem
THING 19
Adding Value
Asymmetric value: outperforming in bad times matters more than great times. Up 100% in a good year + down 50% in a bad year = lose to 0% all years. Marks's Oaktree track record: capture 60% of upside, only 30% of downside. Asymmetric capture = real alpha.
Asymmetric capture
THING 20
Reasonable Expectations
Most common error: expecting too much. Stocks have historically returned 8-10%/year long-term. Anyone promising 20%+ consistently is lying or lucky soon to revert. Set expectations at 7-9% real return. Anything above = bonus from cycle timing or skill (likely temporary).
7-9% expectation
THING 21
Pulling It All Together
Marks's synthesis: "The most successful investors don't have the highest IQ — they have the temperament + discipline to apply a boring framework consistently". The 21 things don't need to be memorised — five need to be internalised: think second-level, value matters, risk = permanent loss, cycle awareness, defence first.
